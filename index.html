Misreading the Crowd: The Composition Externality of Inference

QME 2026

Abstract

When market conditions change quickly, individuals and firms learn from others, using cues such as queue lengths, bestseller lists, usage counts, or the number of active sellers to guide their decisions. Because these aggregate signals are generated by others’ decisions, their informational value depends on how those individuals interpret and respond to market conditions. We study how this feedback shapes market outcomes using 5.7 million decisions by 49,578 New York City taxi drivers at LaGuardia Airport, where passenger demand varies over time and is largely unobserved by drivers. After dropping off a passenger, a driver decides whether to wait in the taxi queue or search for fares elsewhere. Queue length is a driver’s main public signal of airport demand, but it is produced by the decisions of preceding drivers. We use these decisions to classify drivers by their inference rules: sophisticated drivers infer underlying demand from the queue, while roughly 23 percent behave naively, interpreting queue length without accounting for demand. We show that a greater share of naive drivers at the airport lengthens queues when demand is low, shortens them when demand is high, reduces the queue’s informational value, and raises the cost of joining for subsequent drivers. In counterfactuals, an information policy that corrects naive beliefs raises those drivers’ entry into the taxi lot and lowers driver welfare by about 17 percent. In contrast, a uniform entry fee raises driver welfare at every lot capacity we examine and frees roughly 78,000 driver-hours per year, worth about $1.8 million. More broadly, better information need not improve welfare when market signals are endogenous, participants interpret them differently, and access is capacity constrained.

Leveling Down: Competition and Discrimination in Service Markets

NBER Summer Institute (Labor Studies) 2026 · Marketing Science 2026 · WEAI 2025

Abstract

Does competition reduce discrimination? Yes. The standard mechanism is selection, which drives discriminators from the market. But I identify a less benign channel, leveling down: competition narrows disparities by degrading service for everyone, with the largest degradation for those previously treated best. I model discrimination as in-group favoritism rather than out-group hostility. Providers favor same-group customers, but the favoritism requires surplus that competition erodes. Preferential treatment is a luxury good. In the New York City taxi market, airport queues randomly assign passengers to drivers, so discrimination shows up in how a passenger is served, not in whether the driver takes the fare. Before new competitors entered in 2013, drivers charged higher fares and took longer routes for passengers whose race differed from the driver’s. Competition compressed these disparities by half to two-thirds through leveling down. The narrowing carries the model’s signature: the same gap had ignored every within-shift earnings shortfall, and it fell most among the drivers least able to fund the favor. Market-wide, the cost of degraded service exceeded the benefit of reduced discrimination by nearly three to one.

The Price of Identity: Overoptimism and Congruence Concerns

with Eugen Dimant, Lorenz Götte, Michael Kurschilgen, and Maximilian Müller

Abstract

We study how social identity distorts both beliefs and preferences in investment decision-making. We ran framed field experiments in online sports betting with Kenyan and UK soccer fans during the 2021/22 Premier League season, eliciting subjective probabilities and incentivized bets on matches that sometimes involve a participant’s supported team and sometimes do not. Supporters overestimate their team’s win probability by 10–18 percent relative to neutral bettors and allocate about 20 percent more of their budget to bets on their own team than to otherwise similar matches where they are neutral. Embedding these data in a portfolio-choice model, we separate the role of distorted beliefs from that of identity-driven preferences. Distorted beliefs explain 30–44 percent of the excess investment in favored teams, while the remaining 56–70 percent reflects an aversion to profiting when one’s team loses, equivalent to valuing payoffs from such outcomes by 17–27 percent less. Exploiting variation in both recent and season-long performance, we show that this preference to avoid “disloyal” gains is stronger when teams perform poorly. Using the estimated model, we calculate welfare losses from identity-driven distortions of roughly 0.5–2.7 percent of betting budgets, with the exact magnitude depending on whether identity preferences are seen as welfare-relevant or as mistakes.

Defaults and Decisions: Choice Architecture and Consumer Opt-Out

Management Science · Coverage: Forbes, WSJ, Stanford Insights, Marginal Revolution

Abstract

Why do defaults stick? This paper estimates a structural model that disentangles two frictions underlying default adherence: a deviation cost (disutility when the chosen option differs from the consumer’s ideal) and an opt-out cost (effort to override the default). I estimate the model on 8.6 million tipping decisions from NYC Yellow Taxi trips. A stated-preference survey identifies consumer preferences independently of defaults. The central finding is friction complementarity: 84% of default adherence arises from the interaction of both frictions, not from either alone. Deviation costs are small when consumer preferences are near a default, so even a modest opt-out cost sustains adherence. Structural parameters are stable when default options change, ruling out preference anchoring. A single lower default captures 69% of the maximum consumer welfare gain. Tip revenue falls 5.4%.

The Effects of the Affordable Care Act on Seasonal Agricultural Workers

with Jeffrey Perloff · Journal of the Agricultural and Applied Economics Association

Abstract

This study investigates the effects of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) policies (Medicaid expansion, health insurance premium subsidy, and tax penalty) on farmworkers’ health insurance coverage and healthcare utilization. Using the National Agricultural Worker Survey, we find that the ACA policies substantially raised the share of seasonal farmworkers with medical insurance. It significantly increased workers’ use of preventive medical services and decreased the use of hospitals, including emergency rooms, which was a goal of the law’s proponents. These effects did not significantly differ between workers with and without a pre-existing medical condition.
